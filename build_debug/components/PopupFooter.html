Explainer

I was reading the New Yorker article “Is It Wrong to Write a Book With AI?” and keep thinking about how “authorship” and its meaning changes when a new instrument/tool shows up. In the article they refer to the awesome Roland TR-808, which went from confusing novelty in the music world to a foundation of modern sound. Uniquely, the TR-808 had a computer in it which was not the norm for drum machines at the time, according to the article. But, when it went into the marketplace at a prohibitive price (for actual artist/musicians) those who had it could not figure out how to use it, so it was discontinued — and you could get remainders for 90% off the original $1200 price. So — artist/musicans started exploring the new terrain of possibility and changed music production forever. So, one wonders — if computers and pattern-based logic became totally normal in music, why wouldn’t AI do something similar for writing? The real issue isn’t just whether the output is “human enough,” but what we decide creativity means when the process shifts. That’s what makes the question of writing a book with AI feel less like a moral trap and more like a turning point.

So I explored what a role that orchestrates standards for language models in the service of longform journalism might look like in a near-future editorial operations context. Something that felt a bit like what a music producer or studio engineer does in shaping the sonic palette and workflow of music production, but applied to the orchestration of language models and editorial standards in the service of longform journalism.
