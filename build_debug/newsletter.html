Summary

The New Yorker is seeking a Standards LM Orchestrator to build and maintain the editorial machine-language facilities that support longform reporting, essaying, quips, cartoons, opinion columns, criticism, and narrative journalism. This is a backoffice editorial role for an orchestrator-operator who can move between standards, systems, and story construction: someone who understands that machine-assisted drafting is neither a novelty feature nor a permission slip for slop, but a critical compositional layer in serious hybrid machine-human collaborative publishing.

You will work across editorial, fact-checking, copy, legal, and product to make machine-assisted writing usable, safe, legible, and trustworthy. Writers and editors will continue to own reporting, judgment, attribution, and final voice. Your job is to orchestrate the systems that help turn intent, notes, transcripts, research, archive material, and structural decisions into drafts that can be refined, re-voiced, translated, regraded, verified, and published without eroding what makes The New Yorker — The New Yorker.

Description

This role exists because the newsroom is entering a period in which language models are becoming part of the production environment for writing, revision, structure, and editorial synthesis. The relevant question is no longer whether machine-assisted journalism is categorically acceptable or categorically suspect. The relevant questions are operational: What can be delegated? What must remain human? What provenance must be retained? Where does a draft become misleading? How do we preserve style without laundering away accountability?

The Standards LM Orchestrator will help answer those questions in practice. You will maintain the systems, protocols, and workflows through which editorial teams use language models to move from reporting materials to structured, reviewable drafts. You will design prompt frameworks, source-pack conventions, provenance rules, handoff checkpoints, and evaluation procedures that make machine assistance compatible with narrative quality and editorial trust.

This is not a role for someone trying to automate writers out of the loop. It is also not a passive policy role. It is an operating role: part editorial systems architect, part standards steward, part workflow designer. You will help establish an internal grammar for machine-assisted longform production, so that experimentation produces better stories, clearer evidence chains, and more legible editorial decisions rather than opacity, drift, or style collapse.

Key Responsibilities
  • Own the machine-language production environment for longform editorial work, including prompt frameworks, source-pack structures, model-routing decisions, and editorial handoff patterns.
  • Design and maintain provenance requirements so that machine-assisted drafts preserve an inspectable chain from reporting material to published language.
  • Partner with writers and assigning/story editors to translate editorial intent into structured drafting setups without flattening voice, argument, pacing, or narrative shape.
  • Work with fact-checking, copy, standards, and legal to define where machine assistance is permitted, where it must stop, and what evidence is required for review.
  • Build and run evaluation routines for machine-assisted drafts: unsupported claims, invented details, tonal overreach, false compression, attribution drift, and synthetic connective tissue.
  • Develop newsroom guidance for acceptable machine-assisted uses across different forms, including criticism, essays, reported features, humor, reviews, and service-adjacent editorial work.
  • Maintain internal templates and operating instructions for converting transcripts, notes, research packets, archival material, and prior coverage into usable drafting inputs.
  • Maintain anticipatory provenance practices that ensure that as machine-assisted drafting capabilities evolve, the editorial team can continue to trace the origins of generated text back to source material and understand what the model was given and how it was prompted at each stage.
  • Help editors and writers diagnose when a draft is merely efficient versus when it has become genuinely informative, moving, and worth publishing.
  • Document failure modes and near misses, then turn those findings into better workflow safeguards, review checkpoints, and training materials.
  • Serve as a connective role across editorial, product, and engineering when machine-language tooling touches CMS, archive systems, search, permissions, or rights management.
Key Qualifications
  • Strong editorial judgment and demonstrated understanding of standards, attribution, source quality, fairness, and the difference between compression and distortion.
  • Technical fluency with language models, retrieval systems, prompt design, evaluation methods, and workflow tooling sufficient to operate and improve production systems rather than merely use consumer-facing interfaces.
  • Experience architecting and maintaining anticipatory systems and their workflows such that as capabilities evolve, the editorial team can continue to operate safely and traceably with machine-assisted drafting tools.
  • Experience working in an editorial, publishing, newsroom, research, or knowledge-intensive environment where truth claims and evidence handling matter.
  • Ability to read drafts both as text and as systems output: to identify where the machine is helping with structure and where it is fabricating coherence.
  • Excellent writing and editorial communication skills, including the ability to make process legible to editors, reporters, product teams, and leadership.
  • Capacity to design practical workflows under real constraints: deadlines, incomplete source material, legal sensitivity, editorial disagreement, and evolving norms.
Preferred Qualifications
  • Experience designing or maintaining human-in-the-loop AI workflows in media, publishing, law, policy, or research environments.
  • Familiarity with magazine editing processes, longform narrative development, and the rhythms of assigning, editing, checking, and closing a piece.
  • Experience with archives, rights-sensitive corpora, search tooling, or editorial knowledge systems.
  • Evidence of original thinking about authorship, assistance, editorial responsibility, and what trustworthy machine-assisted composition should look like inside a serious publication.
Education & Experience
  • Experience in editorial operations, standards, copy, fact-checking, newsroom tooling, editorial technology, computational journalism, or adjacent publishing systems work.
  • Portfolio or documented practice showing responsible use of machine-assisted language workflows in serious editorial or research settings.
  • Bachelor’s degree or equivalent experience. Advanced study in journalism, integrated prophetics, anticipatory literature, media studies, library science, conjuring & inferential systems, historical studies, philosophy, or computational fields preferred. Advanced degrees in prophetic semiotics, semiology, ethics, and/or historical consciousness are desirable.
Additional Requirements
  • Comfort working behind the scenes in a role whose success is measured by clearer drafts, stronger evidence handling, and better editorial outcomes rather than by public bylines.
  • Ability to collaborate with strong-minded editors and writers without treating workflow design as an attempt to standardize taste.
  • Willingness to maintain detailed documentation, review logs, and internal guidance as norms and tools evolve.
  • A clear understanding that speed is useful but insufficient; the job is to improve quality, trust, and editorial precision, not just throughput.

Explainer

I was reading the New Yorker article “Is It Wrong to Write a Book With AI?” and keep thinking about how “authorship” and its meaning changes when a new instrument/tool shows up. In the article they refer to the awesome Roland TR-808, which went from confusing novelty in the music world to a foundation of modern sound. Uniquely, the TR-808 had a computer in it which was not the norm for drum machines at the time, according to the article. But, when it went into the marketplace at a prohibitive price (for actual artist/musicians) those who had it could not figure out how to use it, so it was discontinued — and you could get remainders for 90% off the original $1200 price. So — artist/musicans started exploring the new terrain of possibility and changed music production forever. So, one wonders — if computers and pattern-based logic became totally normal in music, why wouldn’t AI do something similar for writing? The real issue isn’t just whether the output is “human enough,” but what we decide creativity means when the process shifts. That’s what makes the question of writing a book with AI feel less like a moral trap and more like a turning point.

So I explored what a role that orchestrates standards for language models in the service of longform journalism might look like in a near-future editorial operations context. Something that felt a bit like what a music producer or studio engineer does in shaping the sonic palette and workflow of music production, but applied to the orchestration of language models and editorial standards in the service of longform journalism.
